Continuum robot visual servoing, two ways

A two-segment tendon-driven continuum robot chases 3D targets using nothing but four markers seen by two fixed cameras. The orange controller uses the textbook constant curvature model. The blue one learned from the simulator's less polite physics (backlash, sag, drift) and reports its own uncertainty. Built as a study of the problems in the SDU Robotics job ad on sensing and AI-based control of continuum surgical robots. It is a toy, and the gap between the idealized model and the simulated truth is the point.

Version 3. The simulation is 3D. Version 1 showed it through one 2D camera, and everything that looked wrong followed from that: a clicked point was really a ray, so the controllers reached for any point on a line; the markers seemed to change spacing when only the perspective did; and a target reached from one pose and not from another looked more inconsistent than it was. This version fixes the sensing, the task and the views, and says what each change cost. What changed, and why. Version 1 is kept unchanged at the bottom of the page.

What changed in version 3, and why

1. The simulation is 3D. Version 1 showed it through one 2D camera

The robot bends in three dimensions: two segments of constant arc length (1.0 and 0.8 in normalized units), one constant-curvature arc per segment with a curvature vector that picks the bending plane, three tendons per segment, four markers at fixed arc positions, no joints, no cross-section. Version 1 rendered that through a single perspective camera placed off to the side and gave the controllers a pixel to chase. Three things followed.

The point was a ray. With one camera an image point fixes two of the tip's three coordinates. Depth along the viewing ray was never observed and never asked for, so the loop accepted any point on that line as success. In visual-servoing terms this is the known limitation of a single point feature; for a robot that has to reach a point it is the wrong task.

The markers seemed to move along the robot. A segment bending toward the camera projected long, one bending away projected short, and the tube was drawn wider when nearer, as any camera would. In 3D the chord between neighbouring markers varies by under 4%; in that image it ran from about 1 px to about 60 px. A decorative taper on the drawn tube made it worse. Nothing in the geometry ever changed.

Reaching looked more inconsistent than it was. A pixel is reachable from several bending planes, and a local law picks one greedily. From one pose the loop found a plane that reaches the target; from another it committed to the wrong plane, hit the curvature limit and stalled short. Seen in 2D that reads as random; in 3D it is a controller with a hidden degree of freedom.

2. What version 3 changes for that

Sensing and task. Two fixed, calibrated cameras (CAM 01 side, CAM 02 top) see the four markers; each marker is triangulated and both controllers get the same 3D estimate and a 3D target with a 3D error. The cameras add no noise, so the triangulation is exact; the page does not pretend otherwise.

Placing a target. A click in the inspector still gives a ray. The height plane in the side sensor view turns the ray back into a point; move the plane and the target slides along the same ray.

Views. The inspector orbits the workspace (drag), with side, top and isometric presets; the side and top presets are the sensors' own poses, so what the controllers see can be compared with any other angle. The tube is drawn with one constant radius. Segment ends are filled markers, midpoints hollow rings: two segments, one midpoint each.

3. The boundary

Version 1's dashed outline was the convex hull of the 900 targets the ensemble had been trained on, projected into the image, described as "close to the reachable set". It looked irregular because it was the perspective silhouette of a three-dimensional workspace, sampled finitely, and it was not the reachable set: the model reaches about 35 px further where the robot curls back on itself. Version 3 draws the reachable set itself, in 3D: 200,000 sampled configurations at the curvature limits, their tip positions binned by direction around the cloud's centroid, the largest radius per bin kept, holes filled, smoothed, then scaled up by the smallest factor that keeps 99.9% of the samples inside. That is the translucent envelope in both views. It is an outer envelope, generous where the true set is concave; whether a specific target is reachable is decided by the planner's inverse kinematics, and the trial table says beyond reach when it is not.

4. Inverse kinematics

Version 1 solved none. Both controllers were local laws: the classical one a damped pseudo-inverse of the ideal model's pixel Jacobian at its believed configuration, integrated into a curvature command; the learned one a regressed gain matrix of the same shape. Nothing chose a configuration deliberately. Version 3 keeps local feedback laws on the 3D error (the classical one now differentiates the ideal model for a 3×4 Jacobian; the ensemble was retrained on triangulated markers and a 3D error) and adds a planning layer above both: when a target is set, the inverse kinematics is solved numerically on the ideal model (a coarse global search over 12,000 sampled configurations for those whose tip lands near the target, the one nearest the current configuration refined by damped Gauss-Newton inside the curvature limits; if nothing reaches the target, the closest reachable point), a straight configuration path runs to it at 60% of the actuator rate limit, and each controller tracks that reference with feed-forward plus its own feedback. When the path ends, the loop is the direct law again. The plan is only as good as the ideal model; under payload the feedback term carries the difference.

Is the plan needed with a 3D target? Measured, not assumed: the "Plan first" switch above turns it off, and the table below reports both. With the direct law, the classical controller sent to a workspace-edge target from the rest pose still ends stalled in the wrong bending plane, 115 mm short; the scripted demo opens with exactly that. Over random edge targets (table below) the direct classical law settles 23 of 40 and stalls about 25 mm short on average; under the plan it settles 40 of 40. The learned law is a different story: about 30% of those edge targets lie outside the population it was trained on, it flags them and drops its own authority to 0.3x, and that costs settle rate, 22 of 40 direct and 35 of 40 under the plan, with small steady-state errors when it does settle. Warning and slowing down is the designed behaviour; its price is in the table. On interior targets nothing is flagged, the plan costs the classical controller about 0.4 s of settle time, and the learned controller is marginally faster with it. A null-space term (curvature minimization, or a pull toward a good configuration) was tried before the planner and moved the numbers only slightly, because the failure is a basin, not the redundancy resolution.

What this is, and is not

  • Both controllers see exactly the same thing: four markers triangulated from two fixed cameras. Neither reads simulator state. The inspector is for you, not for them.
  • The two robots are identical simulator instances with the same random seed, so the comparison is fair. Both sit under the same planner, and the planner can be switched off for both.
  • The robot's geometry is fixed: constant arc lengths, constant curvature per segment, markers at fixed arc positions (filled at the segment ends, hollow at the midpoints). What changed on screen in version 1 was perspective.
  • The "truth" simulator is phenomenological: constant curvature plus actuator lag, tendon backlash, gravity droop, inter-segment coupling and optional tendon drift. Its curvature is biased by load, not redistributed along the arc, so it stays in the constant-curvature shape family; a rod model would be the next step, and the model mismatch here is milder than a real tendon-driven robot's.
  • The learned controller imitates a training-time expert that could see the simulator's true Jacobian. At run time it has no such access. Three signals, all set from data, flag extrapolation: the target outside the population its training targets were drawn from (tips at up to 90% of the curvature limits, decided by the same inverse kinematics with the limits scaled to 90%), the feature vector far from the training set, or the five networks disagreeing. Any of them and it says so and lowers its own gain; the plan's feed-forward is not scaled, because it does not come from the networks.
  • The translucent envelope is the outer boundary of the ideal model's reachable set. The truth robot never leaves it; the planner decides reachability point by point.
  • The model is dimensionless. Millimetres on this page are a display convention (robot length 180 mm), not a claim about any device.
  • This page is the whole system: simulator, sensing, planner, controllers, pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope in one file. Training, envelope and evaluation scripts are in the repository.
