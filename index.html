Continuum robot visual servoing, two ways

A two-segment tendon-driven continuum robot chases 3D targets using nothing but four markers seen by two fixed cameras. The orange controller trusts the textbook constant-curvature model; the blue one learned from the simulator's less polite physics and reports its own uncertainty. Built as a study of sensing and AI-based control of continuum surgical robots. It is a toy, and the gap between the idealized model and the simulated truth is the point.

Version 3. The simulation is 3D; version 1 showed it through one 2D camera and gave the controllers a pixel to chase, and everything that looked wrong followed from that. This version fixes the sensing, the task and the views. The three questions it drew, answered. Version 1 is kept unchanged at the bottom of the page.

The three questions, answered

Q1How was the irregular boundary determined?

It was the convex hull of the 900 targets the ensemble trained on, projected into the image: irregular because it was the perspective silhouette of a 3D workspace, and not actually the reachable set. Version 3 computes the reachable set as itself: 200,000 sampled tip positions at the curvature limits, an outer envelope around them (the grey cage), the ensemble's training population beside it (the blue cage), and on the target plane the exact cross-section, holes included. Near the base that section is a ring: a robot of fixed length cannot bring its tip next to its own base. Whether one specific target is reachable is decided by inverse kinematics, and the trial table says beyond reach when it is not.

Q2Is the distance between the joints variable?

There are no joints and nothing varies. Two segments of constant arc length (1.0 and 0.8 normalized), one constant-curvature arc per segment, three tendons each, four markers at fixed arc positions. In 3D the chord between neighbouring markers changes by under 4%; the old oblique camera stretched that to a factor of sixty on screen, and a decorative taper on the drawn tube suggested geometry that was never modelled. The orbiting inspector, the constant-radius tube and the Tendons view now show the fixed structure directly.

Q3How was the inverse kinematics solved?

In version 1 it was not: both controllers were local feedback laws chasing a pixel, and with one camera a pixel is a line in space, so the loop could commit to the wrong bending plane and stall at the curvature limit. Version 3 gives both controllers a real 3D target (two calibrated cameras, triangulated markers) and puts a planner above them: numerical inverse kinematics on the ideal model, a coarse search over 12,000 sampled configurations refined by damped Gauss-Newton inside the limits, then a configuration path that the feedback laws track. The Planner switch turns it off, and the table below is the measured difference: direct, the classical law loses 18 of 40 workspace-edge targets and stalls about 25 mm short, the learned law loses 5; planned, both reach 40 of 40. A null-space term was tried first and barely moved the numbers; the failure is a basin, not redundancy resolution.

Also changed

The robot stands upright with gravity along its axis, so it sags only as it leans and the payload scales that. The Flexibility slider bends the mechanism further and the planner, cages and outline follow; the ensemble is retrained across the whole range (93,600 samples) and flags any target outside what it was ever trained on. Millimetres are a display convention (robot = 180 mm). Version 1's scripted demo froze the page at its last step; fixed here, kept there.

What this is, and is not

  • Both controllers see exactly the same thing: four markers triangulated from two fixed cameras. Neither reads simulator state. The inspector is for you, not for them; hiding a robot hides its drawing, not its simulation.
  • The two robots are identical simulator instances with the same random seed, so the comparison is fair. Both sit under the same planner, and the planner can be switched off for both.
  • The robot's geometry is fixed: constant arc lengths, constant curvature per segment, markers at fixed arc positions (filled at the segment ends, hollow at the midpoints). What changed on screen in version 1 was perspective. The Tendons switch strips the tube and draws what actuates each segment: three tendons at fixed radius (drawn at 2.5 times the model's tendon radius so they can be told apart) with spacer discs at the base, midpoint and end of each segment. Their colour runs from white (slack) to the robot's colour (fully shortened), driven by the simulator's transmitted tendon displacement, so backlash and drift show as tendons that stay coloured after the command has moved on.
  • The "truth" simulator is phenomenological: constant curvature plus actuator lag, tendon backlash, gravity droop, inter-segment coupling and optional tendon drift. Its curvature is biased by load, not redistributed along the arc, so it stays in the constant-curvature shape family; a rod model would be the next step, and the model mismatch here is milder than a real tendon-driven robot's.
  • The learned controller imitates a training-time expert that could see the simulator's true Jacobian. At run time it has no such access. Three signals, all set from data, flag extrapolation: the target outside the population its training targets were drawn from (tips at up to 90% of the curvature limits, decided by the same inverse kinematics with the limits scaled to 90%), the feature vector far from the training set, or the five networks disagreeing. Any of them and it says so and lowers its own gain; the plan's feed-forward is not scaled, because it does not come from the networks.
  • Two cages. The grey one is the outer boundary of the ideal model's reachable set at the current flexibility (the truth robot never leaves it; the planner decides reachability point by point). The faint blue one is the population the ensemble was trained on, tips at up to 90% of the limits at flexibility ×1.8; a target outside it is flagged.
  • The model is dimensionless. Millimetres on this page are a display convention (robot length 180 mm), not a claim about any device.
  • This page is the whole system: simulator, sensing, planner, controllers, pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope in one file. Training, envelope and evaluation scripts are in the repository.
