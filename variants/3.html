A geometric model with backlash and lag on top: a simulated continuum robot

It is built on a geometric model: each of the two segments is one constant-curvature arc of fixed length (Webster and Jones, 2010), so four numbers describe the whole robot, and the simulator adds lag, backlash, sag and drift on top, with invented coefficients. The page is the three layers of that build, what the cameras sense, what the model assumes and what the control law does, and each layer answers one of the questions quoted below.

  1. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras, a side one and a top one. Each marker's 3D position is triangulated from the two images, and that is all either controller gets.
  2. A controller turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one is a small ensemble of networks trained on the simulator with its four effects.
  3. Lengths are shown as if the robot were 180 mm long; the model itself is dimensionless.
Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
truth model
the simulator the controllers are tested against: the ideal geometry plus lag, backlash, sag and drift, with invented coefficients
play operator
a dead band on each tendon: after a reversal the command must travel twice the half-width before the tendon moves again; the element Prandtl-Ishlinskii hysteresis models are built from
inverse play
leading each tendon command by the half-width in its direction of motion, so the dead band is crossed at command time
inverse lag
a lead filter: adding the time constant times the command's rate of change, which undoes a first-order lag
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
click ray
the line in space behind a click on a camera image; the target plane turns it into a point
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model by damped Gauss-Newton inside the bending limits
reachable set
every point the tip can occupy within the limits; its slice at one height is the outline drawn on the target plane
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second of a 6 s trial
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

Questions, and one remark, from a robotics researcher about the first version, paraphrased.

Q1How was the irregular boundary determined? Layer one.
Q2How is the robot modelled, and does the distance between neighbouring markers vary? A continuum structure with continuously varying geometry would be hard to manufacture. Layer two.
Q3How is the inverse kinematics solved? Layer three.
Q4Is the model geometric, or of some other type? The first line of this page, and layer two.

Layer one · sensing

Two cameras, four markers, one point

What this layer knows: pixels in two calibrated cameras. Nothing about the robot's shape.

The boundary question is a sensing question. In the first version the boundary was drawn in one camera image, where it is only a silhouette. Here a click is a ray from the camera you click in, the target plane turns that ray into a point, and the outline on the plane is the reachable set's slice at that height, so it says whether the point is a task at all.

Drag the target plane in the side sensor view, or its slider, then click in the band between the two outlines. The chart below the views lays out the last click ray and marks where the plane cuts it.

Reachable stretches along the click ray
The last click ray laid out by distance from the camera; shaded where it passes through the reachable set; the marker is where the target plane cuts it.
  • Read itinside a shaded stretch the point is a valid task; outside, the trial table says beyond reach. Near the base the ray can enter, leave and re-enter the set, because the set is a ring there.
  • Take awaya click gives a line, the plane gives the point, and the reachable set decides whether the point is a task.

The boundary is the reachable set's slice at the target plane, computed from the model, not drawn from any image.

At the current plane the outline and the inverse-kinematics solver agree on computing percent of 300 random points; the computing that disagree all lie within computing of the outline, inside one 6 mm grid cell of the edge. A click outside the band is refused rather than run. The last click's point was not placed yet.

Layer two · model

Constant curvature, fixed lengths, backlash and lag on top

What this layer knows: the ideal geometry, plus a play width and a lag time constant identified from the cameras. Nothing about sag, coupling or drift.

It is a geometric model. Each segment is one constant-curvature arc of fixed length, three tendons at a fixed radius bend it, and the four markers sit at fixed positions along the arc, so nothing along the robot changes length. On top of that geometry the simulator adds four effects the controllers are never told about. Two of them make the tip's path depend on where the command came from: backlash, a play operator on every tendon, which is rate independent, and a first-order lag on every tendon, which grows with speed. Below, one bend is swept back and forth with no feedback at all, and the tip's path against the command traces the loop.

  • Read itat the same command the rising and the falling pass sit apart; that vertical gap, in millimetres of tip position, is what the numbers above the chart average over the sweep. The loop's horizontal width is the dead band a reversed command must cross before the tip turns.
  • Take awaythe inverse play removes the rate-independent part; what it leaves grows with speed, and inverting the lag as well closes the loop, because this simulator contains exactly those two models. The closed curve still sits computing off the dashed ideal on average: that offset is the static sag and the coupling between segments, which are not compensated here.

It is a geometric model: two constant-curvature arcs of fixed length, four numbers of configuration; the effects are additions with invented coefficients.

Constant curvature is the standard kinematic model and the cheapest one. A Cosserat-rod or finite-element model would add what it leaves out, such as load-dependent shape and friction along the backbone, at a much higher cost per step; this page stays with the arcs and puts the missing physics in the four effects instead.

The arc length is 1.8000 units in every pose. Over 300 random poses at flexibility x1.0 the first marker pair's 3D chord is at most computing percent shorter than the 50 mm of arc between them, because an arc's chord shortens as it bends, not because anything moves along the robot.

Both effects were identified from the two cameras alone. The backlash half-width comes from sweeping one bend at two speeds and extrapolating the dead distance at each reversal to zero speed: computing of bend in segment one per tendon, against the computing the simulator was given. The lag time constant comes from a small step in the command, with the play already taken up: computing against computing. The cameras are noise free and the simulator contains exactly these two operators, so the matches test the code, not a method for real hardware. It is the identify-then-compensate step of a simulation-to-real pipeline, run against a simulator instead of a robot.

On the slow sweep the mean gap is computing without compensation, computing with the inverse play and computing with the lag inverted as well. Even the slow sweep moves fast enough for the computing lag to leave computing after the inverse play. At three times the speed, computing, computing and computing. Here the rate-dependent part is one clean first-order lag, so a lead filter removes it. On a real robot it is not, and that is where a rate-dependent Prandtl-Ishlinskii model or a sequence model such as an LSTM earns its place (Wu and others, 2021).

On a real robot the same identify-then-invert step would run on measured camera data instead of a simulator, and the first test would be whether the identified residual per tendon stays fixed or changes with bend and speed. Where it changes, a fixed operator is the wrong model and a learned residual is the natural replacement.

With the camera loop closed, feedback already hides most of it, at errors below what any real camera could resolve. For the classical law with the planner, no payload and no drift, over 20 seeded interior targets, all settled either way, the steady-state error is 0.24 mm without compensation and 0.02 mm with the inverse play; at twice the estimate it settles sooner (median 0.90 s against 1.85 s) but ends at 0.38 mm, so a wrong width costs accuracy. Layer three · control

Inverse kinematics on the model, tracked by two laws

What this layer knows: the triangulated point and the ideal geometry. Nothing from the simulator.

The first version had no inverse kinematics at all, and adding it changed more than anything else. Both controllers below get the same target, curled under the base. With the planner off they chase it directly; with it on, the inverse kinematics is solved on the geometric model first, by damped Gauss-Newton inside the bending limits, and the same feedback laws track the path to that solution.

Edge targets settled out of 40, direct against planned
From the evaluation run (2026-08-31): four disturbance conditions, both controllers, 40 edge targets per bar.

  • Take awaythe plan lifts the classical law from 22/40 to 40/40 and the learned law from 35/40 to 40/40; under payload and drift both keep most of that.

Above both laws, the plan reaches every nominal edge target; alone, neither law does.

On the target above, direct, the classical law ended running from the target: it follows the local error gradient, bends segment one into a different plane from the one the inverse kinematics finds, and stops where that gradient vanishes, short of a target that is reachable. Planned, it settled in running. In the evaluation run, direct, the classical law stalled 24.5 mm short on average and the learned law 3.9 mm; planned, both sit at 0.4 mm and 0.5 mm.

What this page gets wrong

  • The four effects are invented: their forms are standard (a rate limit and lag, a play operator, a gravity droop, a random walk) but every coefficient was typed by the author, so the numbers describe no device.
  • The backlash is one play operator per tendon with one width, identified by perfect cameras in a simulator that contains exactly that model. On a real robot the loop also depends on friction along the backbone, the bend and the load, and identifying it would need measured data.
  • The lag is inverted by a lead filter that assumes one clean first-order lag, which is only true because the simulator was written that way; on real hardware a lead filter also amplifies sensor noise, and the rate dependence is not one time constant.
  • Sag, coupling and drift have no chart; they appear only in the coefficient table.
  • Direct, the learned law settles 35/40 edge targets; the plan, not the learning, is what reaches all of them.
  • The plan inherits the ideal model's errors and costs about a third of a second on interior targets: median settle 1.48 s direct against 1.83 s planned.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent. The true figure is under 5 percent at flexibility x1.0, about 4.96 percent, and layer two measures it live.

How this page was built

The simulator, sensing, planner, both controllers, the pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope are the version 3 code, loaded unchanged; the identification of the play and the lag and their inverses are one added module. The ensemble was trained by node train/train.js, the evaluation tables by node train/eval.js (2026-08-31) and the closed-loop compensation check by node train/hyst-eval.js (2026-09-25), all with fixed seeds. Every other number on this page is computed in this browser when it loads. Version 1 is kept unchanged at v1.html.
