Before the robot, five things

  1. A continuum robot bends along its length instead of turning at joints. This one has two bending segments of fixed length, like two circle arcs joined end to end.
  2. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras. Each marker's 3D position is computed from the two images, which is called triangulation, and that is all either controller gets.
  3. A controller is a rule that turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one learned from a simulator with lag, backlash, sag and drift added.
  4. The reachable set is every point the tip can occupy without exceeding the bending limits. A target outside it cannot be reached by any controller.
  5. Lengths on this page are a display convention: the model is dimensionless and drawn as if the robot were 180 mm long.

Each act has a hypothesis box with three labelled lines, prediction, baseline and caveat, then a Run button; the verdict is written from the run.

The setup, drawn by the same renderer as every scene below: the base, the two segments, the four markers (filled at segment ends, hollow at midpoints), the two sensors, the target plane and the click ray that places a target on it.

Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
marker
one of four points on the robot that the cameras can see: the middle and end of each segment
chord
the straight-line distance between two neighbouring markers; the arc between them is fixed, the chord shortens as the arc bends
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model within 2 mm
occupancy grid
the reachable set stored as 6 mm cells, filled from 300,000 sampled tip positions; the outline on the plane is its slice
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
protocol
the trial rules shared by the page and the evaluation run: 6 s per target, settle under 5 mm held 0.8 s, steady state over the final second, fixed seeds
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

The three questions this page answers

Asked about version 1 on 29 August, quoted as written.

Q1How did you determine the irregular boundary?
Q2How did you model your robot? Is the distance between each pair of joints variable? In practice, it is usually difficult to physically manufacture a continuum structure with continuously varying geometry.
Q3How did you solve the inverse kinematics of the robot?

The answers given were: the boundary is the computed reachable set; nothing varies along the robot; the inverse kinematics is solved on the ideal model and tracked. Each is tested below.

  • Webster and Jones, 2010, Design and kinematic modeling of constant curvature continuum robots: the constant-curvature arc, the geometry of every segment here.
  • Chaumette and Hutchinson, 2006, Visual servo control: the feedback law, resolved-rate servoing on a point feature.
  • Kuhnen, 2003, Modeling, identification and compensation of complex hysteretic nonlinearities: the play operator, the element the simulated backlash is built from.
Audit one · the boundary

Outline against inverse kinematics

This audit matters because a boundary that disagrees with the solver would make "beyond reach" in the trial table a guess. The claim under test is that the outline drawn on the target plane is the reachable set's cross-section. The test asks the inverse-kinematics solver about random points on that plane and compares its answer with the outline's.

Predictionthe outline and the solver classify at least 95 percent of random points on this plane the same way. Baselinethe outer cage's slice, the boundary anyone could draw from the envelope alone. Caveatthe solver tolerates 2 mm and searches a 12,000-entry table; grid cells are 6 mm, so disagreements should sit on the boundary itself.

Each point is classified by the outline, by the cage, and by the solver.

Audit two · the geometry

Chord and arc length over random poses

This audit matters because "does the distance between joints vary" is answerable by measurement, not by assertion. The claim under test is that nothing moves along the robot. The test draws random configurations and measures, in each, the arc length, the straight-line chord between every neighbouring marker pair, and the same pair's spacing on each camera's screen.

Predictionarc length stays at 1.8 units in every pose; each pair's chord shortens by no more than the geometry allows, which is 1 minus sin(φ/2)/(φ/2) at the pair's bending limit φ, computed from the limits; on-screen spacing varies by more than a factor of two, since a pair pointing at the lens has no length on screen. Baselinethe arc length, the manufactured quantity. Caveatthe chord bound grows with flexibility, because an arc's chord shortens as the arc bends; this audit runs at the current setting, and 500 poses cannot reach the exact bound.

Audit three · the planner

Ten edge targets, direct against planned

This audit matters because the largest claim on the page, against edge targets for the classical law, should be reproducible without leaving the browser. The claim under test is that a feedback law alone loses edge targets a plan above it reaches. The test runs the evaluation protocol on its first ten seeds, both controllers, with and without the plan.

What this page gets wrong

  • Ten trials in the browser check the protocol; they do not replace 40. The two are shown side by side and can disagree by a trial or two.
  • Audit one tests the outline against the solver, not against the truth: both use the ideal geometry, so an error shared by both would pass.
  • Audit two measures the model's geometry, which is fixed by construction; it cannot detect a manufacturing defect the model does not contain.
  • Direct, the learned law still settles only of the edge targets; the plan, not the learning, is what reaches all of them.
  • The truth model is invented and the cameras are perfect; every number here is a floor no real setup reaches.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent; audit two measures it instead of asserting it.
  • Millimetres are a display convention, not a claim about any device.

How this page was built

The simulator, sensing, planner, both controllers, the pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope are the version 3 code, loaded unchanged. The audits run on that code in this browser: audit three uses the same trial routine that wrote the evaluation file (), with the same seeds, 31000 plus seven times the trial number. Every number on this page is read from that file at build time or computed here; none is typed. Version 1 is kept unchanged at v1.html.
