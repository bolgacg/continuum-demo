Geometric model or learned model: what is this controller built on?

The controller on this page is built on a geometric model, and the page is organised as the three layers of that build: what the cameras sense, what the model assumes, and what the control law does with both. Each layer answers one of the questions a robotics researcher asked about the first version, and each states what it is allowed to know. At the end you will know that the boundary is a property of the sensed workspace, that the geometry is the standard constant-curvature model with four invented effects on top, and that the inverse kinematics is solved on the geometric model and tracked by the loop.

Before the robot, five things

  1. A continuum robot bends along its length instead of turning at joints. This one has two bending segments of fixed length, like two circle arcs joined end to end.
  2. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras. Each marker's 3D position is computed from the two images, which is called triangulation, and that is all either controller gets.
  3. A controller is a rule that turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one learned from a simulator with lag, backlash, sag and drift added.
  4. The reachable set is every point the tip can occupy without exceeding the bending limits. A target outside it cannot be reached by any controller.
  5. Lengths on this page are a display convention: the model is dimensionless and drawn as if the robot were 180 mm long.

The setup, drawn by the same renderer as every scene below: the base, the two segments, the four markers (filled at segment ends, hollow at midpoints), the two sensors, the target plane and the click ray that places a target on it.

Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
truth model
the simulator the controllers are tested against: the ideal geometry plus lag, backlash, sag and drift, with coefficients typed by the author
marker
one of four points on the robot that the cameras can see: the middle and end of each segment
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
click ray
the line in space behind a click on a camera image; the target plane turns it into a point
Jacobian
the table of how much each curvature moves the tip; the classical law inverts it to choose a bend
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model
reachable set, cross-section
every point the tip can occupy within the limits; its slice at one height is the outline drawn on the target plane
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
ensemble
five small networks that each propose a command; their spread is a measure of doubt
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second of a 6 s trial
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

Layer one · sensing

Two cameras, four markers, one point

What this layer knows: pixels in two calibrated cameras. Nothing about the robot's shape.

This layer matters because the boundary question is a sensing question: a boundary drawn in one image is a silhouette, and the loop can only target what the sensing can localise. The inspector shows both sensors as small frustums. A click is a ray from the camera you click in; the target plane turns that ray into a point; the outline on the plane says whether that point is inside the reachable set.

Drag the target plane in the side sensor view, or its slider, then click inside the outline. The chart below lays out the last click ray and marks where the plane cuts it.

Layer two · model

Constant curvature, fixed lengths, four invented effects

What this layer knows: the ideal geometry and four numbers of configuration. Nothing about lag, backlash, sag or drift.

This layer answers two questions at once: whether anything varies along the robot, and whether the model is geometric or of some other type. It is geometric. Each segment is one constant-curvature arc of fixed length; three tendons at a fixed radius bend it; four markers sit at fixed positions along the arc. On top of that geometry the simulator adds four effects the controllers are never told about. The one with a visible static mechanism is sag: the robot below holds a leaning pose with its tendons drawn, and the payload toggle loads it.

Flip the Payload switch in the toolbar. The load ramps over about a second; the marker on the chart moves with the current lean and load.

Layer three · control

Inverse kinematics on the model, tracked by two laws

What this layer knows: the triangulated point and the ideal geometry. Nothing from the simulator.

The third question exposed the largest omission, no inverse kinematics in the first version, and this layer shows what the plan adds. Both controllers below get the same target, curled under the base. With the planner off they chase it directly; with it on, the inverse kinematics is solved on the geometric model first and the same feedback laws track that path. The chart holds the evaluation run: 40 edge targets per condition, each controller, with and without the plan.

What this page gets wrong

  • The four effects are invented: their forms are standard (a rate limit and lag, a play operator, a gravity droop, a random walk) but every coefficient was typed by the author, so the numbers describe no device.
  • Sag is drawn from the static map only; the dynamic effects (lag, backlash, drift) have no chart here, they show as the tendons staying coloured after the command has moved on in the Tendons view.
  • Direct, the learned law still settles only of the edge targets; the plan, not the learning, is what reaches all of them.
  • The cameras are perfect and triangulation is exact; the sensing layer's numbers are a floor no real setup reaches.
  • The plan inherits the ideal model's errors and costs about a third of a second on interior targets: median settle direct against planned.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent; layer two measures it live instead of asserting it.
  • Millimetres are a display convention, not a claim about any device.

How this page was built

The simulator, sensing, planner, both controllers, the pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope are the version 3 code, loaded unchanged. The ensemble was trained by node train/train.js, the evaluation tables by node train/eval.js (), all with fixed seeds. Every number on this page is read from that evaluation file at build time or computed in this browser; none is typed. Version 1 is kept unchanged at v1.html.
