Before the robot, five things

  1. A continuum robot bends along its length instead of turning at joints. This one has two bending segments of fixed length, like two circle arcs joined end to end.
  2. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras. Each marker's 3D position is computed from the two images, which is called triangulation, and that is all either controller gets.
  3. A controller is a rule that turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one learned from a simulator with lag, backlash, sag and drift added.
  4. The reachable set is every point the tip can occupy without exceeding the bending limits. A target outside it cannot be reached by any controller.
  5. Lengths on this page are a display convention: the model is dimensionless and drawn as if the robot were 180 mm long.

The scene below is the only one on the page. Scroll the stops on the right and it follows; each stop greys every control but its own, and free play at the end unpins them all.

The setup, drawn by the same renderer as the scene: the base, the two segments, the four markers (filled at segment ends, hollow at midpoints), the two sensors, the target plane and the click ray that places a target on it.

Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
marker
one of four points on the robot that the cameras can see: the middle and end of each segment
chord
the straight-line distance between two neighbouring markers; the arc between them is fixed, the chord shortens as the arc bends
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model within 2 mm
occupancy grid
the reachable set stored as 6 mm cells, filled from 300,000 sampled tip positions; the outline on the plane is its slice
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
pinned control
the one control a stop leaves active; the others are greyed until free play
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

Stop one · where the tip can go

The outline is a slice of the reachable set

This stop matters because every "beyond reach" row in a trial table rests on a boundary, and a boundary someone drew by hand would be a guess. The outline on the plane is the cross-section, at the plane's height, of a grid filled from 300,000 sampled tip positions; the grey cage is the same set's outer envelope. Drag the plane through the base and the outline becomes a ring: a robot of fixed length cannot bring its tip next to its own base.

Pinned control the plane height, dragged in the side feed or with its slider. Then click on the plane to place a target.

Stop two · nothing moves along the robot

Fixed geometry under a moving viewpoint

This stop matters because on a single screen the markers seem to slide along the robot, which is what the second question was about. The robot is held still at a bent pose with its tendons drawn; the only thing that changes while you drag is the viewpoint. The first marker pair's straight-line distance in 3D is measured every frame, and so is the same pair's spacing on the inspector's screen.

Pinned control the orbit: drag in the inspector.

Since you started orbiting
Each quantity as a ratio to its value at the start of this stop, on a logarithmic axis: the 3D chord (grey) and the on-screen spacing (accent).
  • You seetwo lines, each the ratio of a quantity to its own starting value: the first pair's chord in 3D and its spacing on the inspector.
  • Read itthe chord line is flat at one; the spacing line swings by a factor of ten or more as the pair turns toward and away from the lens.
  • Take awaythe appearance of sliding is perspective; the distance between the markers is a property of the model, fixed to four decimals.

Stop three · why a feedback law stalls

Inverse kinematics above the feedback law

This stop matters because the strongest claim on the page, that a plan reaches edge targets a feedback law loses, should be visible on one target. The target sits under the base, at the edge of reach. Direct, the feedback law follows the local gradient into a bend from which the target is unreachable and stops there; planned, inverse kinematics picks the bend first and both laws track the path to it.

Pinned control the Planner switch in the toolbar; flipping it reruns the target from rest.

Tip error, direct then planned
Distance from the classical tip to the target, with the switch marked as an event; the hairline is the 5 mm settle band.
  • You seethe classical law's tip error over two runs of the same target, planner off then on.
  • Read itthe first run flattens tens of millimetres above the band; the second crosses it and stays.
  • Take awaythe stall is a property of the target's basin, not of the law's gain: the plan changes which bend is aimed at.

What this page gets wrong

  • One scene serves three stops, so a stop's state can be undone by a stray click; the Set button restores it.
  • Stop two holds the pose still to isolate the viewpoint; a moving robot changes the chord by a few percent as well, which the verdict's last sentence measures.
  • Direct, the learned law still settles only of the edge targets; the plan, not the learning, is what reaches all of them.
  • The truth model is invented and the cameras are perfect; every number here is a floor no real setup reaches.
  • The plan costs time: a median of about 0.3 s more to settle on interior targets, the price of the detour.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent; stop two measures it instead.
  • Millimetres are a display convention, not a claim about any device.
