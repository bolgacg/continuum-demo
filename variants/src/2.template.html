What does one camera hide about a continuum robot?

Version 1 of this simulation showed a 3D robot through one 2D camera and gave its controllers a pixel to chase. Three questions from a robotics researcher each landed on a consequence of that choice. This page replays each consequence inside the current simulator, then switches to the correction under the same physics, so both can be measured. At the end you will know which of the first version's faults were sensing, which were drawing, and which were control, and what each cost in millimetres.

Before the robot, five things

  1. A continuum robot bends along its length instead of turning at joints. This one has two bending segments of fixed length, like two circle arcs joined end to end.
  2. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras. Each marker's 3D position is computed from the two images, which is called triangulation. A single camera fixes only two of a point's three coordinates; the depth along its viewing ray is not observed.
  3. A controller is a rule that turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one learned from a simulator with lag, backlash, sag and drift added.
  4. The reachable set is every point the tip can occupy without exceeding the bending limits. A target outside it cannot be reached by any controller.
  5. Lengths on this page are a display convention: the model is dimensionless and drawn as if the robot were 180 mm long.

The setup, drawn by the same renderer as every scene below: the base, the two segments, the four markers (filled at segment ends, hollow at midpoints), the two sensors, the target plane and the click ray that places a target on it.

Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
marker
one of four points on the robot that the cameras can see: the middle and end of each segment
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
viewing ray
the line in space that lands on one pixel of one camera; every point on it looks the same to that camera
visual servoing
closing the control loop on what a camera sees; on a pixel in version 1, on the triangulated tip now
Jacobian
the table of how much each curvature moves the tip, in pixels or in space; the classical law inverts it to choose a bend
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model
bending plane
the direction a segment bends toward; a target can be reached from several of them, and a local law picks one greedily
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second of a 6 s trial
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

The three questions this page answers

Asked about version 1 on 29 August, quoted as written.

Q1How did you determine the irregular boundary?
Q2How did you model your robot? Is the distance between each pair of joints variable? In practice, it is usually difficult to physically manufacture a continuum structure with continuously varying geometry.
Q3How did you solve the inverse kinematics of the robot?

All three point at one cause: the simulation is 3D, and version 1 showed it through a single 2D camera and gave the controllers a pixel to chase. Each act below is one consequence.

  • Webster and Jones, 2010, Design and kinematic modeling of constant curvature continuum robots: the constant-curvature arc, the geometry of every segment here.
  • Chaumette and Hutchinson, 2006, Visual servo control: the feedback law, and the known limitation of a single point feature that act one replays.
  • Kuhnen, 2003, Modeling, identification and compensation of complex hysteretic nonlinearities: the play operator, the element the simulated backlash is built from.
Act one · sensing

A point target seen by one camera is a line

This act matters because the irregular boundary and the odd reaching both start here: with one camera the loop was never asked for depth. Below, the classical law chases the same target in two ways. As in version 1, it sees only the side camera and drives the tip's pixel onto the target's pixel. As now, it drives the triangulated tip onto the 3D point. The inspector draws the side camera's viewing ray through the target, the line every point of which looks like the target to that camera.

Flip the switch and the target is re-run from rest under the other rule. The learned controller is absent from this act: version 1's ensemble took single-image features and has no honest equivalent here.

Act two · drawing

Perspective, and a taper that was never modelled

This act matters because "is the distance between joints variable" was a fair reading of what the old view showed, and the answer has to show the same robot in the old view and in the new one. Below, the robot sweeps between two targets under the planner. As in version 1, it is seen through the oblique camera of the first page; as now, through the calibrated side sensor, with the Top chip as the second view. The geometry is identical in both states: two arcs of fixed length, markers at fixed arc positions.

Flip the switch to change the camera the view is drawn through. Version 1 also drew the tube with a decorative taper; that drawing choice is not reproduced, the camera is.

Act three · control

The bending plane a local law picks

This act matters because the third question, how was the inverse kinematics solved, had the answer "it was not", and the cost is measurable. Both controllers below get the same target, curled under the base. As in version 1, they chase it directly and a local law commits to whichever bending plane the gradient points at first. As now, the inverse kinematics is solved on the ideal model and the same laws track that path.

Flip the switch and the target is re-run from rest. As in version 1 means the planner off; as now means the planner on.

What this page gets wrong

  • The "as in version 1" states are reconstructions on the version 3 stack: the same feedback law on a pixel, the same camera pose, the planner off. Version 1 itself is unchanged at v1.html, and its decorative tube taper is not reproduced here.
  • Version 1's learned ensemble took single-image features; it has no honest counterpart in act one, so that act runs the classical law alone.
  • Direct, the learned law still settles only of the edge targets; the plan, not the learning, is what reaches all of them.
  • The truth model is invented: its coefficients were typed by the author, and it stays in the constant-curvature family. The cameras are perfect and triangulation is exact.
  • The plan inherits the ideal model's errors and costs about a third of a second on interior targets: median settle direct against planned.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent; act two measures it live instead of asserting it.
  • Millimetres are a display convention, not a claim about any device.

How this page was built

The simulator, sensing, planner, both controllers, the pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope are the version 3 code, loaded unchanged. The pixel law of act one is version 1's formulation on version 3's side camera: a 2 by 4 pixel Jacobian by central differences and the same damped least-squares step. The ensemble was trained by node train/train.js, the evaluation tables by node train/eval.js (), all with fixed seeds. Every number on this page is read from that evaluation file at build time or computed in this browser; none is typed.
