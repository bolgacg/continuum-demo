How far can a two-segment continuum robot reach, and how does it get there?

This page answers three questions a robotics researcher asked about the first version of this simulation, one chapter each: how the workspace boundary was determined, whether the robot's geometry varies, and how the inverse kinematics was solved. Each answer is computed in front of you rather than asserted. Before the robot, five things

  1. A continuum robot bends along its length instead of turning at joints. This one has two bending segments of fixed length, like two circle arcs joined end to end.
  2. Four markers on the robot are seen by two fixed cameras. Each marker's 3D position is computed from the two images, which is called triangulation, and that is all either controller gets.
  3. A controller is a rule that turns the gap between the tip and the target into a change of bend. The orange one uses the textbook model; the blue one learned from a simulator with lag, backlash, sag and drift added.
  4. The reachable set is every point the tip can occupy without exceeding the bending limits. A target outside it cannot be reached by any controller.
  5. Lengths on this page are a display convention: the model is dimensionless and drawn as if the robot were 180 mm long.

The setup, drawn by the same renderer as every scene below: the base, the two segments, the four markers (filled at segment ends, hollow at midpoints), the two sensors, the target plane and the click ray that places a target on it.

Terms used on this page
constant curvature
the assumption that each segment bends as one circular arc; four numbers then describe the whole robot, a curvature and a bending direction per segment
marker
one of four points on the robot that the cameras can see: the middle and end of each segment
triangulation
computing a point's 3D position from its pixel in two cameras whose poses are known
visual servoing
closing the control loop on what a camera sees, here on the triangulated tip
Jacobian
the table of how much each curvature moves the tip; the classical law inverts it to choose a bend
inverse kinematics
finding the bend that puts the tip on a chosen point, solved here on the ideal model
reachable set, cross-section
every point the tip can occupy within the limits; its slice at one height is the outline drawn on the target plane
planner
solve the inverse kinematics first, then let the feedback law track the path to that solution
ensemble
five small networks that each propose a command; their spread is a measure of doubt
extrapolation flag
the ensemble saying the situation is outside what it was trained on, and lowering its own gain
settle, steady state
settle: the first time the tip error stays under 5 mm for 0.8 s; steady state: the mean error over the final second of a 6 s trial
play operator
the simulated backlash: after a reversal, the first stretch of commanded motion goes nowhere
edge and interior targets
edge targets sit at 95 to 100 percent of the bending limits; interior ones at up to 85 percent

The three questions this page answers

Asked about version 1 on 29 August, quoted as written.

Q1How did you determine the irregular boundary?
Q2How did you model your robot? Is the distance between each pair of joints variable? In practice, it is usually difficult to physically manufacture a continuum structure with continuously varying geometry.

And on 6 September: whether the model is a geometric model or some other type. It is geometric, and chapter two says exactly which kind.

  • Webster and Jones, 2010, Design and kinematic modeling of constant curvature continuum robots: the constant-curvature arc, the geometry of every segment here.
  • Chaumette and Hutchinson, 2006, Visual servo control: the feedback law, resolved-rate servoing on a point feature.
  • Kuhnen, 2003, Modeling, identification and compensation of complex hysteretic nonlinearities: the play operator, the element the simulated backlash is built from.

Chapter two · the geometry

Fixed arc lengths under a moving viewpoint

This chapter matters because one view made the markers seem to slide along the robot, and a reader who believes the geometry varies cannot trust the reachable set of chapter one. Nothing varies: two arcs of fixed length, one constant curvature per segment, three tendons each at a fixed radius, four markers at fixed positions along the arc. The robot below sweeps between two targets with its tendons drawn; orbit it and press Side or Top.

Move the Flexibility slider under the view. It scales the bending limits, the one parameter that changes what the robot can do without changing its geometry; the reach follows, the lengths do not.

What changes with flexibility and what does not
300 random poses per flexibility setting, computed on load; the marker is the current setting.
  • You seeagainst flexibility, the variation over random poses of four quantities, as a percentage of each one's mean on a logarithmic axis: the backbone's arc length, the 3D chord of each neighbouring marker pair, and the first pair's spacing on the side camera's screen.
  • Read itthe arc-length line sits on the floor, it does not vary at all; the chord lines are a few percent and rise with flexibility, because an arc's chord shortens as the arc bends; the pixel line sits far above them, because a chord pointing toward the lens has little length on screen.
  • Take awaynothing moves along the robot; bending and perspective are what the eye saw.

Arc length is constant to four decimals at every flexibility; the chord shortens by a few percent because an arc's chord shortens as it bends.

At the current flexibility the first pair's 3D chord varies by … percent over random poses, the other pairs by … and … percent, while its spacing on the side camera varies by a factor of …. The arc length is … units in every pose, a constant of the model rather than a measurement.

A fixed geometry leaves four numbers to choose, two curvatures and two bending planes; the next chapter asks how to choose them for a target and what happens when a feedback law chooses them alone.

Chapter three · the inverse kinematics

Inverse kinematics above the feedback law

This chapter matters because version 1 solved no inverse kinematics at all, and the measured cost of that omission is the largest number on the page. Both controllers below get the same target, curled under the base where a local law is tempted into the wrong bending plane. With the planner off they chase it directly; with it on, the inverse kinematics is solved on the ideal model first and the same feedback laws track that path.

Flip the Planner switch in the toolbar. Each flip re-runs the same target from the same rest pose, so the two traces are comparable.

  • You seethe distance from each controller's triangulated tip to the target over the last 16 seconds, with the 5 mm settle band as the hairline and each switch flip marked.
  • Read ita line that flattens above the band is a stall, the controller parked at its bending limit in the wrong plane; a line that crosses the band and stays is a settle.
  • Take awaydirect, the classical law stalls short of this target; planned, both controllers reach it.

A feedback law alone loses edge targets; a plan on the ideal model reaches them.

Over 40 edge targets in the evaluation run, direct, the classical law settled 22/40 and stalled 24.5 mm short on average; planned, 40/40. The learned law alone settled 35/40; planned, 40/40. A null-space term was tried first and barely moved the numbers; the failure is a basin, not redundancy resolution.

Both laws still carry the truth model's lag, backlash, sag and drift; the model card and the failure pane say how much, and the free-play scene at the end has the toggles.

Verdict
ahead of the classical law on edge targets and under payload, level under payload with drift, slightly behind under drift alone. The extrapolation flag fired on 0 percent of steps in every table cell, so those runs do not test it; only a target beyond the reach on this page does.

What this page gets wrong

  • Direct, the learned law still loses 35/40 of the edge targets read the other way round: it settles that many, not all.
  • Under payload with drift, planned, interior targets settle 34/40 (classical) and 33/40 (learned) with steady states near 4 mm, close to the band.
  • The truth model is invented: its coefficients were typed by the author, and it stays in the constant-curvature family. A rod model would be the next step, and the mismatch here is milder than a real tendon-driven robot's.
  • The cameras are perfect and triangulation is exact; a real setup adds pixel noise and calibration error.
  • The plan inherits the ideal model's errors and costs about a third of a second on interior targets: median settle 1.48 s direct against 1.83 s planned.
  • The outer cage over-states the reachable set near the base, where the set is a ring; the outline on the plane comes from the occupancy grid instead, which is why chapter one draws both.
  • An earlier version of this page said the marker chord varies by under 4 percent; computed here it is about 5 percent at flexibility ×1.0 and grows with flexibility, so the number is now computed, never typed.
  • Millimetres are a display convention, not a claim about any device.

How this page was built

The simulator, sensing, planner, both controllers, the pretrained weights and the precomputed envelope are the version 3 code, loaded unchanged. The ensemble was trained by node train/train.js (about 45 minutes), the reachable envelope and grid by node train/workspace.js, the evaluation tables by node train/eval.js (about 6 minutes, 2026-08-31), all with fixed seeds. The numbers in the headline lines and the chapters are either read from that evaluation file at build time or computed in this browser on load; none is typed. Version 1 is kept unchanged at v1.html.
